The science of a waterproof wall.

WetBond was built from first principles: how water actually moves through concrete, and what it takes to stop it there. This page walks through the physics, the chemistry of the bond, and the evidence behind each claim.

See the technical data

Concrete is a sponge with a hard shell

Every concrete wall is full of channels you can't see.

Concrete is cement, sand, stone and water. Only part of the water is used to harden the cement; the rest dries out over weeks and months, leaving a network of microscopic channels called capillary pores.

Most are far thinner than a hair, but there are a great many, and many connect, forming paths from the soil side of a wall to the inside face. Cracks, the wall-floor joint and form-tie holes are simply larger versions of the same thing.

In plain terms: concrete is hard, but it isn't sealed. Water can travel through it.

Three ways water moves through a wall

Pushed, pulled, and drifting.

Pushed: hydrostatic pressure

Water in saturated soil presses on the wall at about 0.43 psi per foot of depth: roughly 3.5 psi at the base of a wall with soil saturated 8 feet up. Only depth matters; a soaked yard and a nearby lake push equally. It isn’t much force, but it never lets up, and it drives water into any opening. Water rising under the slab is a different case; there, your dealer checks whether the job also needs a way to relieve it.

Pulled: capillary action

Thin channels pull water in, the way a paper towel draws up a spill. The thinner the channel, the stronger the pull, so concrete’s fine pores can move water sideways and even upward with no outside pressure. That’s why a wall can be damp in a dry season.

Drifting: vapor diffusion

Water also moves as vapor, an invisible gas drifting from the humid side toward the drier side. It makes no puddles, but it carries moisture through anything unsealed and builds pressure behind anything sealed.

Why holding water on the surface is hard

A coating is only as strong as its grip.

A coating sits on the face of the wall, held by an adhesive bond a few thousandths of an inch thick. Water arriving through the pores stops behind that film, and three forces work on the bond line at once: steady water pressure, minerals crystallizing behind the film, and vapor pushing out. None is dramatic. Together, without letup, they peel coatings off.

That's the reason the industry concluded you can't stop water from the inside. It's a conclusion about surface films. It doesn't describe a material that isn't on the surface.

The chemistry of the bond

Reactive penetration, covalent bonding.

Reactive penetration

WetBond is two liquids mixed on site that react with each other, and it’s also water-reactive: responsive to the moisture in concrete, engineered to go after it rather than fight it. Applied to the inside face, it’s drawn into the pores by the same capillary pull that draws water in, and reacts with the moisture it meets. That’s why damp concrete helps, and why it works overhead: at the scale of a pore, capillary pull beats gravity.

WetBond Core and WetBond Nano carry no pigment, which would clog the fine channels; for a finished color, WetBond Color and Color UV are the tinted members of the family.

For concrete in the sun, WetBond UV is aliphatic. Sunlight yellows and chalks many coatings by attacking their ring-shaped (aromatic) molecules. Aliphatic molecules don’t have those rings, so WetBond UV is UV stable by chemistry, not by protectants that wear out.

Covalent bonding

Hardened cement isn’t inert: its pore walls are lined with hydroxyl groups left by the hardening reaction. WetBond forms covalent bonds with those sites, true chemical bonds rather than the surface grip of glue or paint.

A coating relies on its grip; WetBond relies on the concrete.

Stopping water, letting vapor breathe

Waterproof is not the same as airtight, on purpose.

A material that stopped all vapor would trap it inside the wall, and because WetBond is bonded into the concrete, that pressure would act on the concrete itself. So WetBond is semi-permeable: tight enough to stop liquid water and wicking moisture, open enough to let vapor pass slowly.

WetBond is designed to let vapor pass.

Words worth knowing

Capillary pore
A microscopic channel left in concrete as excess mixing water dries out. The main path water takes through a wall.
Hydrostatic pressure
The push of water in saturated soil against a wall. It rises about 0.43 psi per foot of depth.
Capillary action
The pull that draws liquid into thin channels, even upward. Stronger the thinner the channel.
Covalent bond
A chemical bond in which atoms share electrons. The kind of bond that holds molecules together.
Hydroxyl group
An oxygen-hydrogen pair (OH) on the surface of hardened cement. The sites WetBond bonds to.
Perm
The unit for how easily water vapor passes through a material. Lower means tighter.
